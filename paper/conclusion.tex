A per-person mixture predictor beat order-1 Markov prediction on every trace tested, and a measured cost-benefit rule decides when to act on it. The timing results are mixed. Against a shared campus resolver, local DNS warming made repeat API calls faster (\V{r3.warm.paired}\,ms per call) and first calls slower (paired difference \V{r3.cold.paired}\,ms). In Chrome, with one training visit per site, the extension rarely acted and showed no gain in the $2\times2$ test, while in the small benchmark with a hovering user (\V{pb.extension.n} sites) its median wait was \V{pb.extension.med}\,ms against \V{pb.default.med}\,ms for Chrome's default. The open question is the one the method is built for: whether weeks of one person's history move enough predictions over the threshold to matter. The tools, their \texttt{stats} and popup holdout figures, and the public code make that measurable on a real machine.
