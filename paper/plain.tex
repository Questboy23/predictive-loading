{\Large\textbf{Predictive Loading in one page}}\hfill Reed Ostrov, Denison University, November 2026\\[-2pt]
\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}

\textbf{The idea.} When you click a link, the waiting starts at the click. Your computer has to find the website, then download the page. People open the same sites in the same order. If the computer can guess your next click, it can do the waiting while you are still reading. Predictive Loading is two small free programs that learn your habits on your own computer and try exactly that.

\textbf{What actually happens when you click.} Two waits, one after the other:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Finding the address (about 5 to 100 thousandths of a second).} Computers find each other by number, so a name like \texttt{youtube.com} must be looked up first. This is the internet's phone book, called DNS. Your Wi-Fi network runs the phone book server. Your computer remembers recent answers for a few minutes, then forgets them and must ask again.
\item \textbf{Downloading the page (about 1 second).} The site sends the page in many pieces, and the browser draws it.
\end{enumerate}
A concrete case: every morning you open your email, then the inbox. Without help, both waits happen after each click. With a correct guess, the phone book answer is already remembered and the inbox is already downloaded, so the page appears almost at once.

The \textbf{Mac tool} handles step 1 of the click (finding addresses) for every app. The \textbf{Chrome extension} handles step 2 (downloading pages) on sites you use often, using a preloading feature Chrome already has.
Both learn only from your own history. Nothing leaves your computer, and page addresses are stored scrambled. Each acts only when the expected time saved is bigger than the cost of a wrong guess (wasted data). Seven free security scanners found no problems.

\textbf{What those numbers mean in real life.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mac tool:} it saves about 0.2 thousandths of a second per lookup. One person makes roughly 900 lookups a day, so that is about 0.2 seconds a day on this fast campus network. You would not notice it.
\item \textbf{Where it could matter:} the saving grows with a slower phone book: about 1\,ms per lookup at a typical home router, 3.6\,ms on a slow connection (estimates from the same data).
\item \textbf{Chrome extension:} about 0.1 seconds faster per next page (\C{e3.next.hl}\,ms), on a page that takes about 1.1 seconds. That is a noticeable snap if real. The test had too much noise to rule out zero, because only 42 sites were tested on a busy machine.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Bottom line.} The guessing works. The Mac tool gives a real but tiny gain on a fast network. The Chrome extension downloaded two thirds of next pages early and was about 0.1 seconds faster, but that is not yet proven. The next step is a larger Chrome test on a quiet machine, and real users, whose built-in comparison measures the saving on their own computers.
